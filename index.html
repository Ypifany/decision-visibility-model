You already decided. Can you see the decision?

Many of the decisions that shape an outcome happen long before the moment that looks like the decision. This page lets you check how well you can see one of yours.

The words behind this text are decisions people make before the big one. Move your pointer or finger to look around.

How the Decision Visibility Model works

DVM does not grade a decision as right or wrong. It looks at the distance between what you expected and what reality is showing you. That distance is information, not failure. It moves in three steps, always about one decision at a time.

  1. Look back

    What were you betting on, what happened, and where is the gap?

    You can try this below.

  2. Look at now

    What do you really have, what did you hand off, and is this still the right problem?

    Done together in sessions.

  3. Look ahead

    What is your new bet, what will you watch, and what will you do?

    Done together in sessions.

The Clarity Check

Four short questions about one decision. You will see whether you could notice it going off track in time to act.

Question 1 of 4

Which decision do you want to look at?

Pick one decision you made or need to make. It can be about work, a tool, AI, money, or your life. Most people start by asking whether they made the best decision. That is a fine place to start.

Who or what helped you decide?

Choose all that apply.

Why did you choose it?

Most big decisions have more than one reason, and some are quiet ones. Choose all that apply.

If this started to go off track, how would you know?

Name up to three things you would watch. For each one, choose how you would find out about a change. If you cannot think of any yet, leave them blank. That tells you something too.

    Look back

    This is the first of the three steps, done on your own. Facts come before meaning, and nobody is grading this. Write as much or as little as you like.

    Step 1 of 6

    What were you trying to create?

    Before the tools and the plan, what situation did you want? If it worked out exactly the way you wanted, what would be different six months from now?

    How were you looking at it?

    Every situation has a frame, the story you use to explain what is going on. The frame decides what looks important. What did you believe the problem was? What were your tools and people being used for?

    What were you betting on?

    A bet is not an accusation. It is simply what you acted as though would happen. Finish the sentence as many times as you need to.

    What actually happened?

    Keep this part plain. Write what you could point to, then sort each one. None of these kinds of information is bad. The risk is mistaking one for another.

    • Fact: Can you point to it, like a number, a date, or an event?
    • Opinion: Whose judgment is this, and what did they see?
    • Assumption: Are you treating this as true without checking?
    • Belief: Do you hold this no matter what happens?
    • Feeling: What is your gut telling you, and about what?

    Where is the gap?

    The gap is the distance between what you expected and what you saw. It does not tell you what to change. It tells you where to look. Notice anything that matched, too.

    What can you see now?

    What can you see now that you could not see when you started this page? Then name one thing you will do in the next seven days.
