You already decided. Was it the best one?

Before any big decision, you make a lot of small ones. You choose who to trust, what to check, and what to let a tool or a person handle. Those small choices shape how things turn out, and they are easy to lose track of. This page introduces a process that helps you see what you were counting on, notice sooner when things start to drift, and make your next decision with more of the picture.

Take the Clarity Check Four questions. About two minutes.

The words behind this text are some of those small decisions. Move your pointer or finger to look around.

Is this for you?

The Decision Visibility Model works for one person or for a whole team. It is for you if any of these sound familiar.

  • You use AI or an AI agent at work, and you want to stay the one in charge of what it does.
  • You chose a security control, a scan, or a framework, and you are not sure it is doing what you think it is.
  • You hired a person, a contractor, or a vendor, and the results are not what you expected.
  • You sign off on decisions other people prepared, like a policy, a risk, or a system going live.
  • You run a program or a training, and you want to know if it is doing what people believe it does.
  • You started a business or a big project, you have been at it a while, and it is not turning out the way you planned yet.
  • A decision turned out badly, and you want to understand it without blaming yourself or anyone else.
  • You are about to make a big decision, and you want to see what you are betting on first.

Why look at a decision?

Every day you hand parts of your decisions to something or someone you trusted. You use an AI tool or agent, a security control, a checklist, a vendor, a policy, or a person you hired. That is how work gets done, and it is not the problem.

The problem is what happens when something goes off track. Most people find out the hard way, through a complaint, a bad review, lower profits, a failed audit, or a meeting with the boss. And when it goes wrong, nobody asks the person or thing you trusted. They ask you.

  • See trouble sooner

    Notice a decision drifting while you can still change course, before it turns into a surprise.

  • Show how you decided

    Be able to say what you chose, what you handed off, and what you were counting on. People can see that you are the one driving.

  • Make the next call easier

    Each look back shows you something you could not see before. A wrong call becomes one you can learn from and recover from.

Lots of training teaches you to do things faster with AI. This is for what comes after: staying in the driver's seat as more of your work runs on its own.

DVM does not decide for you, and it does not promise a perfect outcome. It works like a flashlight in a dark room. You still choose, with more of the picture.

What the process looks like

DVM looks at one decision at a time. It moves around a loop in three steps. Each time around, you see a little more.

Where DVM comes from

DVM stands on ideas from many traditions.

  • Sankofa, an Akan principle from Ghana: go back and get what you need to move forward.
  • Carlos Matus of Chile, who treated every plan as a bet made by a real person in a real situation.
  • Sohail Inayatullah of Pakistan and Australia, who showed how the way you frame a problem shapes the answers you can see.
  • Hansei, Toyota's practice from Japan of honest reflection without blame.
  • The U.S. Army's After Action Review, which looks at what happened with the same honesty.

The Clarity Check

Four short questions about one decision. You can pick one that is behind you, one that is still playing out, or one you have not made yet. It takes about two minutes.

    A short look back

    Three questions, done on your own. Short answers are fine. Nobody is grading this.

    Question 1 of 3

    What were you hoping this decision would do?

    Think about the situation you wanted, not the plan. If it worked out exactly the way you wanted, what would be different?

    What were you betting on?

    A bet is not an accusation. It is what you acted as though would happen. For example: "I acted as though my reviews were being checked the same way I checked them."

    What happened?

    Write up to three things. Then choose what kind of information each one is. If you are not sure, say so. Many things that happen are a fact and someone's judgment mixed together.
